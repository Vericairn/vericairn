## Security

Vericairn 0.9.2 corrects seven weaknesses: how admissions and an estate's root of trust are read, which principal and authority records give roles and scopes, policy authority after an operator role is removed, the permissions of per-user state on Linux, the matching of compatibility forms against held work and blocked terms, estate reloads by requests that had not authenticated, and the .NET runtime every 0.9.1 distributable embeds.

**Admissions count from the first authority outward, and the root of trust from the manifest or witnessed history (presumed 0.4.x to 0.9.1; FIX-102).** An admission or revocation now counts only when the device that signed it was admitted, at that time, through admissions that count in turn, from the estate's first authority, only when its signature verifies, and only when its author is an operator or a person holding `administer` at its time; the judgment no longer depends on the order records are read in, and one that would unseat what its own signature or its author's authority rests on counts for nothing and unseats nothing. Before 0.9.2, a signed admission counted for admission whatever its signer's own standing, and an admission by an author who may not admit, such as an agent, was only reported (`admission.author`) while it still admitted the device. The root operator is now read only from the charter the estate's manifest names, and the first authority, the root operator's first device admitting itself, only from the admission the manifest names (`firstAuthority`, which 0.9.2 writes) or the only such admission. History checkpoints only confirm them: a charter or self-admission that comes with checkpoints of its own, in an estate that has adopted or not, never takes their place, and leaves the copy unsettled, with no record counting (`history.root-ambiguous`), until it is removed; after adoption a manifest naming others, one key or both, does the same instead of moving the root of trust. Before 0.9.2, the charter with the lowest id among those that supersede nothing named the root operator, and the earliest-dated self-admission was the first authority, so another such record could take either place; the estate check reported that record, and the estate's own devices then reported errors. Before adoption the manifest still decides, the manifest is not signed, and unsigned principal and grant records dated before the charter required signatures count; adopt checkpoints after upgrading to close those gaps. Estates whose admissions were all written by the root operator, or by operators and persons holding `administer` through signed records as they stood at each admission's time, and that hold one first charter and one self-admission of the root operator's device, such as the first-party estate, read exactly as before. Upgrade every reader of an estate, since earlier releases refuse a manifest that names its first authority; run `vericairn estate upgrade`, which names it, then `vericairn estate check`, and review any new `signature.unadmitted`, `signature.invalid`, `admission.author`, `admission.unseats-support` or `manifest.first-authority` finding. These cases now admit or revoke nothing and are reported as `admission.author`: an admission or revocation dated before its author held the operator role or `administer`, or after they lost it; one signed by an agent holding `administer`; one by a holder of `administer` whose principal record is described as a person only by an unsigned record no checkpoint anchors; and, in an estate that has adopted checkpoints, one whose author's appointment or grant is unsigned and anchored by no checkpoint. Admit or revoke the device again as the root operator or another person who may now, or, for an unsigned appointment, grant or person record, have the root operator or a person holding `administer` anchor it in a checkpoint, which makes earlier admissions count again; a new signed revision counts only from its own time. A revocation reported as `admission.unseats-support` revokes nothing: write it again as of a time after the admissions it rests on, or from a device whose admission does not rest on the device it revokes; the runtime now refuses to write such a revocation, or an admission whose author may not admit, and says why. An admission that counts only because its author's role or grant rests on an unsigned record no checkpoint anchors is the warning `admission.author-unanchored`: review that record, and adopt checkpoints to anchor what you keep. If `manifest.first-authority` is reported, name your first device's self-admission in the manifest's `firstAuthority`; the finding lists each candidate with its device. If `history.root-ambiguous` is reported, compare the copy with a head known elsewhere (`estate check --expect`) and remove the history that does not belong. An estate whose manifest names no first authority and that holds a second self-admission of the root operator's device, stray or forged, adopted or not, reads `manifest.first-authority` with no device admitted, and `vericairn estate upgrade` refuses it, as it refuses any estate with errors, until you name your first device's self-admission in the manifest by hand.

**Records whose authors lacked the authority they need still gave roles and scopes (all releases through 0.9.1; FIX-110).** Before 0.9.2, a principal's own revision of its principal record could give it the operator role, a principal holding `administer` could give the operator role, and a principal or authority record whose author lacked the authority it needs still gave the roles and scopes it named, with at most a validation finding. From 0.9.2 such records give nothing, and roles and grants are read from the root operator in time order, by every rule that reads them. It required the principal to hold an admitted device.

**Policy authority outlasted a removed operator role (0.8.0 to 0.9.1; FIX-111).** Before 0.9.2, policy authority read an author's operator role at the time a record stated, so a person whose operator role had been removed, still holding an admitted device, could record a bounded policy grant dated into the time they held the role and use it. From 0.9.2 the runtime writes principal and authority records at its own time, policy authority requires the role to stand now or a checkpoint by the root operator or a standing operator to place the record before its removal, a policy record without its required approval or authority counts for nothing, and the runtime writes a checkpoint before a removal, or refuses one that would make records fail where it cannot. Policy authority was introduced in 0.8.0. Remedy for estates on earlier releases: when removing a person's operator role, revoke their devices and their bounded grants at the same time, and review the principal, grant, policy, exception and writing records they authored.

**The Vericairn home and the live store could be readable by other local users on Linux (0.1.0 to 0.9.1; FIX-104).** On Linux, a command that ran the embedded runtime, with no daemon, created the Vericairn home and each estate's live store with the process's default permissions, typically 0755 for directories and 0644 for files, so another local user could read the live store's record bytes, which for a local-only estate are its canonical records. A daemon narrowed the live store's directory from 0.6.1, but not the home or the files. In 0.9.2 the home, every directory down to the live store's, the live store with its `-wal` and `-shm` files, a local-only estate's object store, the batch journals and the keyword search index are owner-only from the moment they exist, whichever process creates them, and a command that opens the estate narrows ones left wider. A live store that belongs to another user is refused; on a filesystem without Unix permissions, such as exFAT, the store is used and a warning says its permissions cannot be restricted. macOS keeps per-user state under `~/Library`, which other users cannot enter, and Windows under the user's profile; both were per-user before. Upgrade, then run any command against each estate, such as `vericairn status`, to narrow existing state. Found in OPS-11's independent review.

**Compatibility forms could hide a held key or a blocked term (0.9.0 and 0.9.1; FIX-105).** The disclosure hold's push scan and the blocked-terms check said that Unicode compatibility forms cannot hide a match, but the shipped builds run without ICU, where the platform's normalization does nothing, so a held work order's key or phrase, or a blocked term, written in full-width letters, with a ligature such as `ﬁ`, or in circled or mathematical letters was not caught; ordinary text, in any case, was. In 0.9.2 both checks fold text with Vericairn's own Unicode NFKC and case folding, from pinned Unicode 16.0.0 tables, identical in every build, so those forms are matched and unrelated text still is not. Estates that use `"disclosure": "held"` or a blocked-terms policy should upgrade; no configuration changes. From 0.9.2, evaluating a blocked-terms rule also reports records written after the policy began that hold a term in such a form. Pushes made with an earlier release are not rescanned: review any public push made while a hold was in force if it may have carried such forms. Found in CONTEXT-02's independent review.

**Requests that had not authenticated could make the runtime reload the estate repeatedly (0.2.0 to 0.9.1; FIX-106).** Request authentication read a device's standing by reloading the whole estate under the runtime's lock before the request's signature was checked. A peer that could reach a signed listener, the local-network listener where it is enabled or loopback from the same machine, could therefore keep the runtime busy and hold up every other operation without authenticating; on a large estate each such request took over a second. In 0.9.2 standing is read from a model the runtime keeps for the records as they are and reads again only when they change, so such requests no longer reload the estate, while a revocation still counts from the next request. Estates with the local-network listener configured should upgrade; no configuration changes. Found in MCP-03's independent review.

**The distributables embedded a .NET runtime with published advisories (0.9.1; FIX-115).** Every 0.9.1 command line and Workbench archive embeds .NET and ASP.NET Core runtime 10.0.14, which five advisories published with .NET's security release of November 10, 2026 affect: CVE-2026-114146, CVE-2026-114147, CVE-2026-114149, CVE-2026-109476 and CVE-2026-109475. In 0.9.2 every distributable is built on .NET SDK 10.0.404 and embeds runtime, ASP.NET Core and apphost 10.0.15, which fixes them; the release's bill of materials lists the embedded packs. The NuGet tool uses the .NET runtime installed with the SDK, which its user keeps current. Releases before 0.9.1 embed older runtimes, which 0.9.1's Security section names as affected.

- Records someone adds, with forged history of their own, never take the root operator or first authority, adopted or not; they can leave a copy unsettled until they are removed. - Settling several first-authority candidates means naming one in the manifest by hand; a command for it is a follow-up.
- Re-judging mutual revocations grows faster than linearly in the number of revoking pairs; only those who may admit can cause it, and a cost test bounds it.
